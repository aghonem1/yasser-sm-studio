A word can be handled with care or thrown without thought. The Mirror opens with one of the most thrown-about words in religious speech: kufr.

Chapter One begins at the root. The Arabic k-f-r carries the sense of covering or concealing something that is already there. In older Arabic usage a farmer could be called a kāfir, because he places a seed in the ground and covers it with soil. The book points to Surah al-Hadid (57:20), where the word is used of cultivators and says nothing about what they believe.

From that root comes the chapter’s central observation: only what is present can be covered. The Qur’an’s own usage often follows this order. In Surah al-Baqarah (2:89) the truth came to a people, they recognised it, and then they rejected it. Rejection came after recognition.

So the book separates two things many of us blur. “Non-Muslim” describes an outward identity that anyone can see. Kāfir, read through its root, raises questions no one can see: what actually reached this person, how it was presented, whether they understood it, and whether they knowingly turned from it. Someone may be turning away from a distorted picture of Islam, and the author adds that the picture may have been painted partly by Muslims themselves.

The chapter anchors this in divine justice: “We would never punish until We had sent a messenger” (Surah al-Isra, 17:15). If the One who knows every heart makes the truth clear before holding people to account, we who see only the surface have little ground for quick verdicts.

“Truth does not need cruelty to protect it.”

None of this asks a Muslim to be less certain of the faith. The chapter is explicit that one may believe Islam is true, explain it and invite others to it. What it takes away is the claim to see inside another person.

### To sit with

- When did I last use a religious label for someone I had never really spoken to?

- What picture of Islam have the people around me been shown, and how much of it came from me?
