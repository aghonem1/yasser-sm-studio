A reflection from My Prayer

For whom am I standing?

Between hearing the call and starting the prayer comes the intention. My Prayer treats it as something simpler, and deeper, than a sentence to get right.

Yasser Fares16 November 20262 minute read

Between hearing the call and beginning the prayer there is a small step that many of us turn into a struggle. The second chapter of My Prayer is about that step, the intention, and it begins by taking the difficulty out of it.

Niyyah, the chapter says, is not a hard sentence to be assembled correctly. It is the heart knowing what it is doing and turning to Allah alone. If you rise knowing which prayer you are about to offer, and meaning to obey your Lord, the intention is already there. The book adds a gentle warning: repeating it over and over can open a door to anxiety, when the aim was only for the heart to gather itself calmly.

Then the chapter asks the question underneath the form.

“For whom am I standing?”

Not to look righteous to other people, it answers, and not to admire oneself. One stands because Allah deserves worship, and because one is poor and in need of His guidance and mercy. The book does not pretend that awareness of other people never creeps in. When it does, the advice is to bring the heart back, gently, to its direction, with the words of al-Fatihah: “You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help.”

The chapter then turns the whole matter around. Answering the call is not a favour the servant does for Allah. It is a gift, and gifts are met with thanks. The book counts how many ears hear the call and do not move, how many bodies are able and do not rise, how many hearts know the road and are held back by delay. So if you find yourself walking towards wudu, it says, thank the One who called you and then enabled you to answer.

Getting ready, in this chapter, is a quiet move from distraction into presence: closing the doors of disturbance that can be closed, and ending what does not need to go on. The book is honest that thoughts will still come. People remember, worry and wander. The difference it draws between heedlessness and presence is only that the present heart comes back each time it notices it has left.

That is why the chapter ends by widening what intention means. It is not one moment at the start. It is a thread, taken hold of again and again until the prayer is done.

To sit with

  • Before the next prayer, can I ask plainly: for whom am I standing?
  • When my mind wanders in prayer, can I return without scolding myself, and count the return as the intention renewed?

Drawn from My Prayer: Chapter Two, “Answering the Call — Intention and Response”.
